\begin{frame}{Status as of July 10}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Key finding so far:} when patients pull their data out of the training,
the damage depends on \emph{who} leaves, not on \emph{how many} leave.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Random departures, even 70\% of all patients: almost no damage.
  \item One whole hospital leaving: \textcolor{mCoral}{\textbf{a 6$\times$ bigger drop}} ---
        worse than losing the same number of patients spread at random.
\end{itemize}
\item But \textbf{one loose thread} was left open, flagged honestly in the report:
\end{itemize}
\vfill
\begin{alertblock}{The doubt}
All of those numbers came from a \emph{simple} model. Maybe the damage only
\emph{looked} small because a simple model is \textbf{too blunt to feel it}.
\end{alertblock}
\vfill
{\footnotesize\textit{Every future result builds on these numbers --- so the doubt had to be
settled before anything else gets built on top.}}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{This week's question}
\begin{block}{The test}
The \textbf{whole withdrawal study} was run again, this time with a \textbf{stronger
model} --- a small neural network --- to see whether the damage gets bigger.
\end{block}
\vfill
Two possible outcomes, \emph{both} useful:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The damage \textbf{grows} $\Rightarrow$ the true cost of letting patients withdraw
      is higher than previously reported.
\item The damage \textbf{stays the same} $\Rightarrow$ the finding holds \textbf{no matter
      which model is used} --- it is a property of the problem itself.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{How the test was kept fair}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\textbf{Same everything, except the model}
\begin{itemize}\small
\item Same 100k patients, same 3-hospital setup, same withdrawal patterns,
      same 5 repetitions.
\item The old and new models were run \textbf{side by side} under identical
      conditions --- 200 training runs in total.
\item The neural net plugs into the \emph{same} training setup
      (no special treatment).
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
\textbf{Two sanity checks}
\begin{itemize}\small
\item The old-model half of the rerun \textbf{repeats the published numbers
      exactly} --- the machinery is identical.
\item The new model is \textbf{genuinely stronger}, not a weakened stand-in
      (next slide).
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vfill
{\footnotesize\textit{So any difference between the two sets of results can only come
from the model --- nothing else changed.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading that chart}
\begin{alertblock}{The answer}
\textbf{The damage stayed the same.} Gray and orange bars match, within the error bars,
in every condition. The published numbers are \textbf{real}, not a side effect of using
a simple model.
\end{alertblock}
\vfill
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The finding got stronger, not weaker.} ``Withdrawals are cheap unless they
      change \emph{who} is in the data'' now holds for two very different kinds of model.
\item \textbf{The headline result survives.} A whole hospital leaving still hurts more
      than losing the same number of patients at random --- and far more than everyday
      come-and-go withdrawals.
\item \textbf{Why nothing grew:} this prediction task is hard for \emph{every} model ---
      the best published scores top out around 0.70 --- so no model has much room
      to fall further.
\end{enumerate}
\vfill
{\footnotesize\textit{One new observation for the write-up: when one hospital holds nearly
all the sick patients, the neural net's starting accuracy is slightly more fragile than
the simple model's.}}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What this means for the thesis}
\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{accuracy half of the thesis is now finished and trustworthy}: the
      damage mechanism, its direction, and its size have all been confirmed on two
      kinds of model.
\item The one-line summary for the dissertation: \emph{letting patients withdraw is
      cheap --- unless the withdrawals remove a particular group of patients, which
      costs 3--6$\times$ more, no matter which model is used.}
\item That was the last open question on the accuracy side. Everything that remains
      is on the \textbf{cost side}.
\end{itemize}
\vfill
\begin{block}{Deliverables this week}
The new neural-net model, the side-by-side rerun of all 200 training runs (fully
repeatable), and the results notebook --- all pushed to the repository.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Next steps}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Measure the blockchain cost} (now unblocked, the main build): every time
      a patient grants or withdraws consent, the blockchain records it --- and that
      recording has a price (fees, waiting time, data traffic). This measurement gets
      wired onto the existing training loop; the withdrawal patterns already built
      generate the traffic.
\item \textbf{Put the two costs together} --- accuracy lost vs money and time spent ---
      into one cost-vs-benefit map (the central result of the thesis).
\item \textbf{Scale test} with synthetic patients (Synthea) to find the point where
      recording every consent change on the blockchain stops being practical.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The key finding was put through a \textbf{stress test with a stronger model} ---
      it \textbf{survived unchanged}, so it is a property of the problem, not a quirk
      of the setup.
\item ``\textbf{Who leaves matters more than how many leave}'' is now confirmed on two
      kinds of model, with identical data and withdrawal patterns.
\item The accuracy side of the thesis is \textbf{done}; next up is measuring the
      \textbf{blockchain cost} side.
\end{enumerate}
